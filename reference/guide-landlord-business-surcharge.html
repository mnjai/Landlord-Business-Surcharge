The Landlord Business Surcharge, explained without the jargon

If you own property in Trinidad & Tobago and collect rent on it, there is a tax you now owe that did not exist two years ago. It doesn't care where in the world you live. Here is the whole thing, in plain language.

Ebenezer Real Estate Services · Updated August 2026

For most of the last decade, the tax question hanging over Trinidad property was Property Tax — a charge based on what a property could theoretically rent for, whether or not anyone was actually paying you a cent. It was unpopular, endlessly delayed, and for owners abroad, mostly a rumour.

The Finance Act 2025 repealed it outright. In its place came something narrower but much sharper: the Landlord Business Surcharge, which took effect on 1 January 2026 and is charged on the rent you actually receive.

If you are reading this from Brooklyn, Scarborough or Croydon and you have an apartment downstairs in Trinidad bringing in a few thousand a month, this applies to you. That is not a technicality — it is the explicit design of the rule.

Who has to register

The Inland Revenue Division's position is short and leaves very little room: you are a landlord if you receive rent. All landlords must register, whether they live in Trinidad & Tobago or outside it.

Three things people commonly assume will get them out of it, which do not:

  • Living abroad. The obligation attaches to the property, not to your address. A house in Chaguanas creates the same duty whether you sleep in Diego Martin or Queens.
  • Being paid in US dollars. IRD addresses this directly: receiving rent in foreign currency for a T&T property still requires registration and quarterly payment.
  • Someone else collecting for you. The definition of landlord expressly includes an agent of the landlord. If a relative or an agency collects your rent, that does not remove you from the register — and it puts them in scope too.

"Rent," for this purpose, is drawn broadly: all income received for the use or occupation of premises. Residential and commercial alike.

The exemptions, in full

The exempt list is short and specific. You are outside the surcharge if you are the State, a State-controlled enterprise, a hotel already paying Hotel Accommodation Tax, or an ecclesiastical, charitable or educational institution of a public character. The Minister can add to that list by Order.

IRD's guidance ends that section with a sentence worth quoting in spirit: if you are not on the list, you must register. There is no small-landlord exemption, no single-unit exemption, and no non-resident exemption.

What it actually costs

The surcharge is charged on your gross quarterly rental income in two bands — 2.5% on the first TT$20,000 in a quarter, and 3.5% on everything above that.

Two details matter more than the rates themselves. First, the threshold is quarterly, not monthly, which means it bites at roughly TT$6,667 of rent a month. Second, if you own several units, they are added together before the bands are applied — you do not get a fresh TT$20,000 allowance per property.

You are not being taxed twice

This is the point most owners miss, and it changes how the number should feel. The surcharge is creditable against your final annual tax liability. It is not a second tax layered on top of income tax on the same rent — it is, in effect, an advance instalment of tax you would owe anyway, collected four times a year instead of once.

If your rental income was already being declared properly, the surcharge changes your cash-flow timing far more than your total tax bill. If it wasn't being declared, that is a separate conversation to have with a practitioner, and worth having sooner rather than later.

Registration: the fee, the papers, the deadline

Registration is a one-time process, separate from paying. It costs TT$2,500, and you receive a Certificate of Registration once it's done.

You will need: one form of photo identification for the landlord — and for the agent, if one is acting; the title document for the property; a utility bill showing proof of address; and an authorisation letter if someone is registering on your behalf.

The deadline has passed. It was originally set three months after the surcharge took effect, moved to 30 May 2026, and then extended again to 30 June 2026 after the Finance Minister met with stakeholders. If you are reading this now, you are late — and IRD's own FAQ page may still display the earlier May date, which has caused some confusion.

The penalty for not registering is TT$1,000 for individuals, or TT$2,500 for companies, for each six-month period you remain unregistered. It is not a single fixed fine; it recurs.

The part that catches owners abroad

The registration form is not submitted online. It must be completed on paper, with the supporting documents attached, and physically delivered to one of four Inland Revenue offices: Port of Spain (IRD Tower, 2–4 Ajax Street), San Fernando (52 Cipero Street), Tunapuna (Tunapuna Administrative Complex), or Scarborough, Tobago (Wilson Road).

District offices will not accept it. Someone has to be standing in Trinidad or Tobago with the papers in hand — which is exactly the problem if you live four hours and a plane ticket away.

You can appoint someone to do it for you

This is the most useful thing in the whole set of rules and it is easy to miss: registration through an agent is expressly contemplated. The documents list includes an authorisation letter for precisely that case, and the agent presents their own identification alongside yours.

So an owner in Toronto does not have to fly down. What they need is someone in Trinidad they trust, a signed authorisation, and their title document and identification sent across. That is a solvable problem, and it is one of the things we do.

Miss one, and two things happen together: an additional charge of 5% of the surcharge payable is applied, and interest runs at 15% a year on the unpaid amount. Fifteen percent compounding on a missed filing is the sort of number that turns a small annoyance into a real one over a couple of years of inattention.

One further duty that trips people up: if anything you told IRD at registration changes, you have 30 days to notify them. A change of ownership, a new mailing address, a property sold, an inheritance — all of it starts a 30-day clock.

What to do now, in order

  • Work out whether you're in scope and what you owe. If you receive rent on T&T property and you're not on the exempt list, you are in. Our free check will give you the quarterly figure and your penalty exposure in about ninety seconds.
  • Gather four documents. Photo ID, title deed or certificate, a utility bill for proof of address, and — if someone will act for you — a signed authorisation letter.
  • Register, even though you're late. The penalty recurs every six months. Every period you wait adds to it, and the exposure does not go away by itself.
  • Put the four dates in your calendar. 31 March, 30 June, 30 September, 31 December. The late-payment charges are separate from the late-registration penalty and stack on top.
  • Get proper advice on the back years if your rental income has not been declared. That is a question for a T&T practitioner, and it is better raised by you than discovered by IRD.
