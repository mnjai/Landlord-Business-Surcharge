It usually goes like this. A parent dies. The children are in New York, Toronto and London. Someone locally keeps an eye on the house. Everyone agrees, informally, who gets what. Nobody does the paperwork, because the paperwork requires being in Trinidad & Tobago and nobody wants to spend their two weeks of leave in a registry.

Ten years on, the roof has gone, a tenant can't be put in legally, the house can't be sold, and one of the siblings has since died too — which means the estate now has an estate inside it.

Understanding why this happens is straightforward. Fixing it takes longer than anyone expects, which is exactly why it's worth starting now rather than next year.

## Nobody inherits automatically

Under Trinidad & Tobago law, when a person dies, no one — not a spouse, not a child, not even an executor named in the will — can lawfully sell, transfer or distribute their assets until the Probate Registry issues a **grant of representation**. This is the court's formal authority to administer the estate, and it is governed by the Wills and Probate Act (Chap. 9:03) together with the Administration of Estates Act (Chap. 9:01).

There are three kinds, and which one applies depends on what the deceased left behind:

- **Grant of Probate** — there is a valid will and a named executor willing to act.
- **Letters of Administration** — there is no will. The court appoints an administrator.
- **Letters of Administration with Will Annexed** — there is a will, but no executor able or willing to act.

A note on the name, because it causes real confusion: *Letters of Administration* is not a letter you write. It is a court document issued to you. People leave a registry counter having been told they "need Letters of Administration" and arrive at an attorney's office asking for help drafting one. There is nothing to draft.

### When you might not need one

A grant isn't always required. Where property was held in joint names under a joint tenancy, it may pass to the surviving joint owner outside the estate. Where the estate contains no land, property or shares held in the deceased's sole name, a grant may be unnecessary. Whether that applies to your situation is a question of how the deed was actually drawn — which is why the title search comes first.

## What the grant unlocks

Until it is issued, nothing moves. Banks will not release funds. The Registrar General's Department will not register any deed transferring the property. No sale can complete, because no one has authority to sign as owner.

Once the grant is in hand, land can be **assented** into the name of the executor or administrator, who can then transfer it on to the beneficiaries. That second step is a separate deed, and it is the one families most often forget — a grant alone does not put the house in your name.

## What the process actually involves

1. **Find the original will, if there is one.** The Probate Registry generally requires the original document. A photocopy is admitted only on a successful application supported by evidence, which adds time and cost.
2. **Instruct an attorney.** Where the estate is valued above TT$4,800, the application *must* be made through an Attorney-at-Law. That threshold is low enough that it covers essentially every estate containing a house. Fees may be fixed, hourly, or based on estate value under the schedule to the Wills and Probate Act.
3. **Establish the death and the beneficiaries.** Death certificate, birth and marriage certificates as needed to prove relationships — often the slowest part when family is scattered across three countries.
4. **Value the estate.** Including a proper valuation of the property.
5. **File and wait.** Then, once granted, assent the land and execute the transfer deed to the beneficiaries.

### You do not have to fly down for most of it

Documents can be signed abroad and sent. An attorney in Trinidad & Tobago can carry the application. What genuinely requires someone on the ground is the legwork — searching the title at the Registrar General's, obtaining certified copies, chasing the registry, getting the property valued and photographed, and confirming the house is still standing and unoccupied while the years pass.

## Why the delay costs more than it seems

Three costs compound quietly while an estate sits unadministered.

**The house deteriorates unmonitored.** A Trinidad & Tobago house left empty for a decade in that climate is often worth less than the land it stands on by the end.

**Occupation becomes a legal risk.** Someone occupying land openly and continuously without the owner's permission for sixteen years can apply to extinguish the owner's title. An unadministered estate with nobody watching is precisely the situation that risk was written for.

**Estates nest.** Each beneficiary who dies before the estate is wound up adds another grant to obtain. Families that started with one straightforward application end up with three, sometimes across different jurisdictions.

## Where to start this week

- **Get a title search done** at the Registrar General's Department. You need to know what the deed actually says before anything else — including whether a joint tenancy means no grant is needed.
- **Locate the original will** and the death certificate.
- **Agree among the family who applies** before you instruct anyone. Disputes discovered mid-application are the most expensive kind.
- **Instruct a T&T attorney experienced in estates.** Not the cheapest one — the one most likely to file complete and correct the first time.
- **Have the property inspected and documented** in the meantime, so that a decade of drift doesn't happen while the application is pending.
