This is the question we field most often from families abroad, usually in a slightly anxious tone: *I've lived in Canada for thirty years — am I allowed to own the family land in Tobago?*

Almost always, yes, and with no licence required. The confusion is understandable, because the rule that governs this turns on something people rarely think about in these terms.

## It's citizenship, not residence

The Foreign Investment Act (Chap. 70:07), which came into force in August 1990 and replaced the old Aliens (Landholding) Act, defines a **foreign investor** as an individual who is not a national of Trinidad & Tobago or of another CARICOM member state.

Read that carefully. It says *national*. It does not say *resident*. Where you live, where you pay income tax, how long you have been away, whether you hold a second passport as well — none of that makes you a foreign investor.

So a Trinidadian who has lived in Brooklyn since 1988, holds a US passport, and has not been back in a decade is still a T&T national if they retain their citizenship. They can acquire land in Tobago exactly as someone in Arima can. No licence. No acreage cap.

<Flag title="Where this genuinely bites: the next generation">
  Your children, born and naturalised abroad, may never have held T&T citizenship. If they inherit or buy Tobago land, they may be foreign investors and need a licence — for any parcel, of any size.

  This is worth resolving *before* it becomes an estate matter rather than after. Where children are eligible for T&T citizenship by descent, obtaining it is usually far simpler than a licence application later, and it removes the question permanently.
</Flag>

## The rules for those who are foreign investors

Two different regimes apply depending on which island the land is on.

### Trinidad

A foreign investor may acquire, without a licence, up to **one acre of land for residential purposes** and up to **five acres for commercial purposes**. Above those limits, a licence from the President is required, and the application must set out the proposed land use and satisfy the relevant planning and environmental authorities.

### Tobago

Different entirely. Since **16 February 2007**, under the Foreign Investment (Tobago Land Acquisition) Order, every foreign investor acquiring an interest in land anywhere in Tobago must obtain a licence first — **regardless of size or purpose**. There is no small-parcel allowance.

Within the Designated Development Areas the process is simplified, and licences are generally granted more or less automatically subject to verification of good character. Outside those areas the authorities retain broad discretion, and the process is known to be lengthy in practice.

### Three things people get wrong about the licence

- **It attaches to the person, not the property.** The licence is personal and non-transferable. If you buy from a foreign investor who holds a licence for that land, you must obtain your own — theirs does not come with the house.
- **Payment has to move a particular way.** The consideration must be paid in an internationally traded currency through an authorised dealer — in practice, a T&T bank licensed to deal in foreign exchange. Sending money informally will not do.
- **It is applied for before acquisition, not after.** The licence must be in place prior to the land vesting in the foreign investor.

## What a licence application involves

Applications go to the Ministry of Finance, with the Tobago House of Assembly involved for Tobago land. The supporting documents typically include the applicant's passport (or a certified copy of the bio-data page and the T&T immigration entry stamp), a Certificate of Incorporation for a company applicant, and Police Certificates of Good Character from the authorities in each country of residence.

Where an application is fully complete it may be processed in around twenty working days. That is the official position and it is worth taking seriously in the right sense: **incomplete applications are returned**, which is where the real delay comes from. Practitioners consistently describe the process as slow, and almost all of that is avoidable rework.

<Box title="None of this changes the surcharge position">
  The Landlord Business Surcharge follows the property, not the owner. If you rent out land or a house in Tobago, you must register with the BIR and pay quarterly — licensed foreign investor, T&T national living abroad, or resident. Citizenship affects whether you may *hold* the land. It has no bearing on whether you owe the surcharge on rent from it.
</Box>

## What to do

- **Confirm your citizenship status first** — and your co-owners'. This one fact determines whether any of the rest applies to you.
- **Check whether your children are eligible for citizenship by descent** if Tobago land will eventually pass to them.
- **If you are a foreign investor buying in Tobago**, establish whether the parcel sits in a Designated Development Area before anything else. It substantially changes the timeline.
- **Arrange the funds route early.** The internationally-traded-currency-through-an-authorised-dealer requirement catches people who have already agreed a price.
- **Take advice on your own status.** Whether a particular person or company is a foreign investor is a legal question, and the definition reaches further than individuals.
