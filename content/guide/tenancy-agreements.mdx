Ask an overseas owner for their tenancy agreement and you get one of three answers. There's a document somewhere from 2011. There's a WhatsApp thread. Or there's nothing, because a cousin arranged it and the tenant seemed decent.

Each of those is a problem, and the Landlord Business Surcharge has turned them from a private inconvenience into a compliance exposure — because you are now expected to be able to evidence what rent you received, from whom, and when.

## Two rules that decide the form your agreement takes

Under the Landlord and Tenant Ordinance, **no lease for a term exceeding three years is valid as a lease unless made by a properly registered deed**. A written agreement to let is still valid and takes effect as an agreement — and a person in possession paying rent may be construed as a tenant from year to year — but that is not the same thing as holding the lease you thought you had.

Separately, leases of a year or more must be **stamped**, and leases of three years or more must be **registered**. Stamp duty on a lease is charged by reference to the rent. Your attorney will obtain the assessment; don't estimate it.

The practical implication for most overseas landlords: keep residential lettings to terms under three years, renewable, unless there's a commercial reason to do otherwise. It keeps the instrument simple and avoids an invalid long lease that everyone assumed was fine.

## What the agreement must actually cover

Beyond the obvious rent-and-term, these are the clauses that decide who wins an argument two years from now:

- **The parties, fully identified.** Full legal names, ID numbers, and a forwarding address for each. Not "Tanty's nephew".
- **The property, precisely described** — including which portion, if you're letting a downstairs apartment or a single unit in a building you also occupy.
- **Rent: amount, currency, due date, and method.** Specify bank transfer to a named account. Cash is the enemy of everything else in this article.
- **Deposit: amount, what it covers, and the conditions and timeframe for its return.** The commonest dispute in T&T lettings, and almost entirely preventable in writing.
- **Who pays which utility** — WASA, T&TEC, internet, and any rates or charges. Name each one; "utilities" alone resolves nothing.
- **Repair obligations split explicitly**, with a threshold: tenant handles minor items up to a stated amount, landlord handles structure, roof, plumbing and major systems above it.
- **Inspection rights** — your right to inspect on reasonable written notice. Without this clause, your quarterly inspection is legally awkward.
- **Assignment and subletting** — prohibited without written consent, or you may discover three families where you placed one tenant.
- **Termination: notice periods on both sides, and grounds.**
- **An inventory and condition schedule**, with dated photographs, signed by both parties at the start. This single attachment settles more deposit disputes than every other clause combined.

## Vetting the tenant, which is now partly a legal duty

As an FIU-registered sector, real estate in T&T carries anti-money-laundering obligations. Where an agent is involved in the letting, tenant identification and record-keeping are part of the job, not an optional courtesy.

A proper file contains photo identification, proof of the address they're coming from, employment or income verification, and references from a previous landlord where they exist. Keep it. Records in this area are expected to be retained for years, and the file you cannot produce is the one you will be asked for.

### Why written tenancies protect your title, not just your rent

There is a second, less obvious reason to document every occupation. A person who occupies land openly and continuously without the owner's permission for sixteen years can apply to extinguish the owner's title. A tenant on a signed agreement paying rent has acknowledged your ownership in writing, repeatedly — which defeats that claim outright.

The relative living in your house rent-free with nothing in writing has not. Counter-intuitively, the documented tenant is the safer occupant.

<Box title="What the surcharge now expects of your paperwork">
  The Landlord Business Surcharge is charged on rent actually received, quarterly, and rent means all income received for the use or occupation of premises. To file accurately you need a record of what was received and when — which means receipted, traceable payments rather than cash and memory.

  Owners who move tenants onto bank transfer and a written agreement find the quarterly filing becomes administrative rather than archaeological.
</Box>

## Where to start

- **Put every occupation in writing**, including family arrangements. Especially those.
- **Move rent onto bank transfer** to a named account. This solves the record problem permanently.
- **Do an inventory and condition schedule** with dated photographs, signed by both sides.
- **Have a T&T attorney settle your standard agreement once.** You'll reuse it for years; it's the cheapest legal spend available to a landlord.
- **Keep leases under three years unless there's a reason not to**, and get any longer lease properly registered.
