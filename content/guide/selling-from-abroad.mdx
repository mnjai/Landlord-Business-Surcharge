Selling from overseas is a sequencing problem more than a logistical one. Every stage can be handled without you setting foot in Trinidad. But the stages have to happen in the right order, and the expensive mistake is marketing a property before establishing that you can actually convey it.

## Start with the title, always

Before a photograph is taken or a price is set, get a **title search** done at the Registrar General's Department. You are looking for three things.

First, whether the deed is in the name of a living person who is selling. If the registered owner died, the estate must be administered before anything can transfer — the Registrar General will not register a deed without the grant of representation, and that process runs from months to years. Second, whether there are encumbrances: an undischarged mortgage, a caveat, a right of way, unpaid rates. Third, whether the boundaries on the deed match what is physically on the ground, which on older Trinidad titles they often do not.

Every one of those is fixable. None is fixable quickly, and all are much cheaper to fix before a buyer is waiting.

<Flag title="The most common reason a remote sale collapses">
  An agreement is signed, a deposit is paid, and only then does anyone search the title — and find the deed is still in a grandparent's name. The buyer waits three months, then walks. The property goes back on the market with a stale listing and a story attached.

  A title search costs very little and takes days. Do it first, every time.
</Flag>

## The power of attorney

The instrument that makes a remote sale work is a **power of attorney** — a document by which you appoint someone in Trinidad to act on your behalf, including to execute the conveyance.

Several things about it are worth getting right:

- **It has to be properly executed where you are.** Signed before a notary public, and typically legalised for use abroad — an apostille where the country is a party to the Hague Convention, or consular legalisation where it is not. Your T&T attorney will tell you exactly which applies to your country; the requirements differ and getting it wrong means doing it twice.
- **It needs to be specific enough.** A power drawn too narrowly won't cover a step that comes up; drawn too broadly, it hands more authority than you should be comfortable giving.
- **Choose the attorney-in-fact carefully.** This person can bind you. Most owners appoint their instructed T&T Attorney-at-Law rather than a family member — not for lack of trust, but because an attorney carries professional obligations and insurance that a cousin does not.
- **It should be registered in Trinidad** before it is relied upon for a conveyance.
- **Have it drafted by your T&T attorney** and executed abroad, rather than drafted abroad and hoped upon. A power that doesn't meet T&T requirements is discovered at the worst moment.

## Pricing a property you haven't seen recently

Owners abroad tend to misprice in both directions — anchored to what the area was worth when they left, or to what a relative said a neighbour got. Get a **professional valuation**. It costs little, it settles family disagreement about the number, and any buyer using a mortgage will require one anyway.

Condition matters as much as location. A house that has stood empty for years usually sells better after modest remedial work — clearing vegetation, fixing the roof, a repaint — than as-is at a discount. Buyers price visible neglect pessimistically, assuming worse underneath.

## Running the sale

1. **Title search and any remedial work** — including probate if the estate is unadministered.
2. **Valuation**, and a decision on pre-sale repairs.
3. **Instruct an attorney and execute the power of attorney**, allowing several weeks for notarisation, legalisation and registration.
4. **List and market** — photographs, viewings conducted by your agent, offers relayed to you.
5. **Agreement for sale**, deposit held by the attorney.
6. **Buyer's searches and financing**, then the Deed of Conveyance stamped and registered. Stamp duty is the buyer's cost, not yours.
7. **Completion and remittance** of the net proceeds.

### Getting the money out

Plan the funds route at the start, not at completion. Moving substantial sums out of Trinidad requires a documented, lawful chain — the bank will want to see where the money came from, and a clean file makes the difference between a transfer that takes days and one that takes months. This is covered properly in our guide on [remitting funds home](/guide/remitting-rent).

## Two things to close out afterwards

**Tell the BIR.** If you were registered for the Landlord Business Surcharge, a change to the information you gave at registration must be notified within **30 days**. Selling the property is such a change. Don't let a closed rental keep an open obligation attached to your name.

**Settle the final quarter.** Surcharge is charged on rent actually received, so rent collected up to completion still falls due on the ordinary quarterly date. Reconcile it before the file is closed rather than after.
