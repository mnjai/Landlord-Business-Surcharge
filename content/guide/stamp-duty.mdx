Stamp duty is a tax on the transfer of property, governed by the Stamp Duty Act and administered by the Inland Revenue Division. It is paid by the **buyer**, not the seller, and it must be settled before the Deed of Conveyance can be stamped.

That sequencing is the part to internalise. An unstamped deed cannot be registered, and an unregistered deed does not give you title anyone will recognise. This is why an attorney collects stamp duty funds from you as part of closing rather than letting it be dealt with afterwards.

Duty is charged only on the portion of the value above the applicable threshold, in bands — like income tax. A property is not taxed at the top rate on its whole value because it crosses a line.

Because the band rates published by third parties frequently disagree with each other and have changed several times, we don't reproduce a rate table here. Your attorney will obtain an assessment from the Stamp Duty Section before payment, and that assessment is the figure that matters. Ask for it early, not at closing.

### First-time homeowner: three details that catch people

- **The deed date governs.** The Deed of Conveyance and any Deed of Mortgage must be executed on or after 1 January 2019. A property bought in 2018 whose stamp duty was never processed does not qualify.
- **Every joint purchaser must qualify.** If one buyer is not a first-time homeowner, the whole purchase drops to the TT$850,000 exemption. This catches parents helping a child buy.
- **It does not apply to bare land.** The first-time exemption covers a house, or house and land together. Buying only residential land falls under the separate TT$450,000 allowance.

Owner-occupation is not required for the first-time exemption. And claiming it does not prevent you claiming the separate TT$25,000 allowance for income tax purposes.

<Flag title="If you change the use of the property, the exemption can be clawed back">
  Where an exempted residential property is put to a non-residential use within one year, the full exempted duty becomes payable. Between one and two years, half becomes payable.

  The duty to tell the BIR sits with **you**, the purchaser on whose deed the exemption was granted, and it runs for two years. Failing to notify attracts a fine of TT$15,000 on top of the duty itself. Turning the downstairs into a shop or a short-let is exactly the scenario this is aimed at.
</Flag>

## The costs people forget

Stamp duty is the largest single line, but it is not the whole bill. Budget for the rest:

- **Legal fees** — commonly quoted in the region of 1% to 2.5% of the price plus VAT, depending on the attorney and the complexity of the title. Inherited or long-unregistered property sits at the higher end.
- **Valuation** — required by any lender, and sensible in any event.
- **Title search** at the Registrar General's Department.
- **Mortgage deed stamp duty**, where there is a mortgage — a separate charge from the conveyance.
- **Agent's commission**, ordinarily borne by the seller.
- **Registration fees** and the cost of certified copies.

Taken together, closing costs commonly add several percent on top of the purchase price. Plan on the higher end of any range you're quoted, particularly if the title needs work before it can be transferred.

## Where this bites for overseas owners

**Deeds of gift are still stamped.** Transferring property to a family member for no money does not make it free of duty. Duty is assessed on value, not on what changed hands. Families "putting the house in the children's names" are often surprised by the assessment.

**Payment is made in Trinidad.** Documents are presented at the Stamp Duty Section and payment is by cash, certified cheque or LINX debit drawn on a T&T bank account. There is no route to settle this from a foreign bank account directly, which is one more reason the transaction needs someone acting for you on the ground.

**Have the assessment done before you commit.** Documents should be assessed for stamping before cheques are drawn. Getting the number wrong at closing delays registration, and a delayed registration on a property you are selling from abroad is expensive in ways that compound.
