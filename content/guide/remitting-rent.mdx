Rent accumulates in a Trinidad account in TT dollars. You live in Toronto and think in Canadian dollars. The gap between those two facts is where most of the friction in remote landlording sits — and very little of it is about the exchange rate itself.

## The foreign exchange reality

Trinidad & Tobago's foreign exchange market has been under sustained pressure for years. The rate against the US dollar is managed and moves within a narrow band, so the headline number is stable. Availability is the variable that actually affects you.

In practice this means commercial banks ration foreign currency. Requests are queued and sometimes partially filled. Larger conversions take longer to arrange than smaller routine ones. Banks prioritise established customers with clear documentation over walk-in requests.

None of this makes moving your rent home difficult. It makes it something to **schedule** rather than something to do in a hurry. The owner who converts on a regular quarterly rhythm through a bank that knows them has a very different experience from the one who turns up needing US$40,000 by Friday.

<Flag title="The single most useful habit">
  Convert and remit on a fixed schedule — quarterly, aligned to your surcharge filing — rather than when you happen to need the money. Predictable, documented, modest-sized transfers from a customer the bank knows are the ones that go through smoothly.

  Availability and requirements change. Confirm current conditions with your bank before planning around any particular figure or timeframe.
</Flag>

## Use an authorised dealer

Foreign currency dealing in T&T is regulated. Conversions and outward transfers go through an **authorised dealer** — in practice a commercial bank licensed to deal in foreign exchange under the Exchange Control Act.

This matters beyond compliance. The informal alternatives people are offered — someone who will "hold" your TT dollars and pay you US dollars abroad — carry real risk: no recourse, no receipt, no record you can show the BIR or your own tax authority, and potential exposure under anti-money-laundering law. The saving on the spread is never worth it.

## Documentation is the whole game

Banks on both ends will ask where the money came from. That is not suspicion of you personally; it is what anti-money-laundering rules require of them, and Caribbean jurisdictions are under particular scrutiny.

The overseas landlord who cannot evidence the source of funds is the one whose transfer sits in review for weeks. The one with a tidy file is processed routinely. What that file should contain:

- **Proof of ownership** — the registered deed in your name.
- **A written tenancy agreement** identifying the tenant and the rent.
- **A traceable payment record** — bank transfers from the tenant, not cash deposits of unexplained origin.
- **Your BIR Certificate of Registration** for the Landlord Business Surcharge, and evidence of quarterly payments.
- **A statement reconciling** rent received, expenses paid, surcharge remitted and net funds transferred, for each period.

<Box title="The surcharge turns out to be an asset here">
  Registration and quarterly filing produce exactly the documentary trail that banks want to see: a government-issued certificate confirming you are a registered landlord, and quarterly payments corroborating the income you say you're earning.

  Owners who were nervous about registering often find it makes the money side easier, not harder. Compliance and liquidity point the same direction.
</Box>

## Don't forget the tax authority where you live

Trinidad rental income does not become invisible because it was earned abroad. The United States taxes citizens and residents on worldwide income; Canada and the United Kingdom tax residents on foreign income subject to their own rules. Foreign account reporting obligations may also apply — in the US, FBAR and FATCA thresholds catch a lot of people who own a modest rental back home and have never thought of themselves as having offshore assets.

Double taxation relief generally exists in some form, and the surcharge is creditable against your T&T annual liability. But how the credits interact across two systems is a question for a qualified adviser in your country of residence. Take that advice; the cost of getting it wrong is disproportionate to the cost of asking.

## Practical steps

- **Keep a dedicated T&T account** for the property. Rent in, property expenses out, nothing else. Mixed accounts are the enemy of clean documentation.
- **Insist on bank transfer from tenants.** Cash rent is the root of most remittance problems.
- **Build a relationship with one bank** and use it consistently.
- **Remit on a schedule**, aligned to the quarterly surcharge dates.
- **Keep every receipt and statement.** Retention periods in this area run to years, not months.
- **Get advice at home** on how the income and the accounts should be reported where you live.
