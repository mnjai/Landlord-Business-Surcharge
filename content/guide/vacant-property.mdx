Most overseas owners worry about the wrong thing. They picture someone breaking in overnight and refusing to leave. That is a trespass, and it is dealt with relatively quickly if you act.

The real risk is slower and much quieter: someone begins using your land, nobody objects because nobody is watching, and sixteen years later they have a legal claim to it that you cannot defeat.

## What adverse possession actually is

Under the Real Property Limitation Act (Chap. 56:03), a person who occupies private land openly, continuously and without the owner's permission for **sixteen years** can apply to the High Court to be recognised as the legal owner. For State land the period is thirty years.

The owner's title is extinguished by court order. **No compensation is paid.** The doctrine rests on the policy that land should not lie unused and unclaimed indefinitely, and it was confirmed for Trinidad & Tobago by the Privy Council in *Ramnarace v Lutchman* [2001] UKPC 25.

A claimant has to establish two things, held together for the full period:

- **Factual possession** — actually using and controlling the land as an owner would, openly and visibly. Casual or intermittent use does not count. Cutting across a yard, or occasionally parking on vacant land, is not possession. Fencing it, farming it, or building on it is.
- **Animus possidendi** — the intention to possess the land as one's own, to the exclusion of everyone else including the true owner. Conduct proves this: maintaining the property, building on it, paying the utilities.

And the possession must be **continuous**. A gap that is more than trivial resets the clock to zero.

## What defeats a claim

This is the useful part, and it is more within your control than most owners realise.

The most powerful defence is **acknowledgement of your title**. If the occupier accepts at any point that the land is yours — by paying rent, signing a tenancy agreement, or even saying so in a letter — the claim generally fails, because they have not been possessing the land as their own.

This has a startling practical consequence. A relative living in your empty house rent-free for twenty years with no documentation is a far greater legal risk than a tenant paying you TT$500 a month on a written agreement. The tenant has acknowledged your title in writing, every month, with a paper trail. The relative has not.

### A separate thing worth not confusing

The government's Squatter Regularisation Programme is a distinct scheme concerning State land. It is not adverse possession and it does not apply to your privately held property. People conflate the two constantly.

## Deterioration, which is the slower loss

Adverse possession is dramatic and rare. Decay is undramatic and near-certain.

A house standing empty in the Trinidad climate does not simply age — it degrades on several fronts at once. Roofs and guttering go first, and once water is entering, the interior follows fast. Termites and rot move through untreated timber. Vegetation lifts foundations and blocks drainage. A water tank and pump left unused for years usually needs replacing rather than servicing. Copper and fittings disappear from unwatched houses.

None of this announces itself. It is discovered, years later, by a family member who finally visits and finds the repair bill exceeds what the house is now worth.

There are second-order costs too. Insurers take a dim view of unoccupied property and cover may lapse or be voided if you haven't disclosed the vacancy. Rates and utilities left unpaid accumulate. And a neglected house makes the whole street's problem visible, which does not help when you eventually want to sell.

## What protection actually looks like

The defence against both risks is the same, and it is unglamorous: **documented, regular presence**.

1. **Physical inspection on a schedule** — monthly is right for an empty property. Someone walks the boundary, checks the structure, notes what has changed.
2. **Dated photographic records** every visit. This is the evidence that matters if a claim is ever made, and it is what turns "we looked after it" into something a court can weigh.
3. **Written permissions.** Anyone using the land — a relative, a neighbour grazing animals, someone parking — does so under a signed, dated document acknowledging your ownership.
4. **Boundaries maintained.** A fence in good repair is both a physical deterrent and evidence of control.
5. **Rates, utilities and insurance kept current**, with the vacancy properly disclosed to the insurer.
6. **Act immediately on any occupation.** The clock only runs while you do nothing. Early, documented objection is cheap; a claim at year fifteen is not.
